Compound growth happens when the returns on an investment are reinvested and then earn returns of their own. In the first years the effect is barely visible. After a few decades it dominates.

## A simple example

Suppose you invest 10,000 at an average annual return of 7%.

| Year | Balance | Growth that year |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10,700 | 700 |
| 10 | 19,672 | 1,287 |
| 20 | 38,697 | 2,532 |
| 30 | 76,123 | 4,980 |

In year 30 the portfolio grows by almost half of the original amount in a single year, without any new money being added.

## The rule of 72

To estimate how long it takes money to double, divide 72 by the annual return. At 7%, money doubles roughly every 10 years. At 4%, it takes about 18 years.

## Why starting early wins

Two investors each put away 500 a month at 7% a year:

- **Maya** starts at 25 and stops at 35, contributing for only 10 years.
- **Daniel** starts at 35 and contributes for 30 years until 65.

By 65, Maya's ten years of contributions have usually grown to a balance similar to Daniel's 30 years. Her money simply had more time to compound.

## Compounding works against you too

A 1% annual fee looks small. Over 30 years it can consume more than a fifth of your final balance. That is why [expense ratios](#/learn/expense-ratios-and-fees) matter so much.
