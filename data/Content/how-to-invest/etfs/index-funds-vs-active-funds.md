## Passive: owning the market

An **index fund**, either an ETF or a mutual fund, buys every security in an index in the same proportions. It doesn't try to outsmart anyone, so it needs no expensive research team and trades rarely.

## Active: trying to beat the market

An **actively managed fund** employs managers who pick securities they believe will outperform. Some succeed in a given year. The challenge is doing it consistently, after fees.

## What the evidence shows

Long-running scorecards that compare active funds with their benchmarks have repeatedly found that a large majority of active funds lag their index over 10 and 15-year periods. Few of the winners in one period stay winners in the next. The main reason is cost: every manager has to beat the market *plus* their fee just to break even with an index fund.

## When active can make sense

- Less efficient markets, such as some small-cap or emerging-market segments
- Strategies an index can't replicate easily
- When you have strong conviction in a manager's process *and* the fee is reasonable

## A practical approach

Many investors use a **core-and-satellite** structure: 80–90% in broad index funds as the core, with a small satellite of active funds or individual stocks. You can compare how actively managed Israeli funds tilt their holdings on our [Exposure Analysis](#/fund-exposure/mor-israel-ta125) pages.
