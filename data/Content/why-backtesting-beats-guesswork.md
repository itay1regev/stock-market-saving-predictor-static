Most investing decisions get made on vibes. Someone reads a headline, feels confident (or scared), and adjusts their portfolio accordingly. Backtesting is the antidote to that: it replaces "I think this would work" with "here is what actually happened when someone did this."

## What backtesting actually tells you

A backtest takes a strategy — an asset, a starting amount, a contribution schedule — and replays it against real historical prices. Instead of imagining how an investment might have grown, you get the actual numbers: the ending balance, the annualized return, the worst drawdown along the way, and how long recovery took.

This matters because intuition is bad at two things in particular:

- **Estimating compounding.** Most people underestimate how much a modest monthly contribution grows over 10+ years, because linear thinking doesn't capture exponential growth.
- **Estimating pain.** It's easy to say "I can handle a 30% drop" in the abstract. It's different to see that a real drawdown lasted 18 months and erased three years of contributions before recovering.

## Backtesting isn't a crystal ball

It's important to be precise about what a backtest is *not*. A backtest tells you how a strategy performed over one specific historical path. It does not guarantee that path will repeat. Markets that delivered a 10% CAGR over the last decade won't necessarily deliver 10% over the next one.

What a backtest is good for is **stress-testing assumptions**. If your plan only works when everything goes well, a backtest across a period that includes a real crash (2008, 2020, 2022) will expose that quickly. If your plan survives those periods and still ends up ahead, that's meaningfully more evidence than a gut feeling.

## How to use a backtest well

1. **Pick a long enough window.** A 2-year backtest mostly tells you about 2 years of luck. Look at 10+ year windows when you can, and check multiple starting points.
2. **Include contributions, not just a lump sum.** Most real investors add money over time. A backtest that only models a single upfront deposit misses how dollar-cost averaging changes the outcome.
3. **Look past the ending balance.** Two strategies can end at the same balance with very different journeys — one with a smooth ride, one with a stomach-churning 40% drawdown in the middle. Check the volatility and max drawdown figures, not just the final number.
4. **Compare against a simple baseline.** Before trusting a complex strategy, check how a plain index fund would have done over the same period. Often, it's a tough benchmark to beat.

Backtesting won't tell you the future. But it will replace guesses with evidence, and that alone tends to produce better decisions.
