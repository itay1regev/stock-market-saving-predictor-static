Most performance summaries lead with the happy numbers: total return, CAGR, ending balance. A drawdown chart tells the other half of the story — how far the investment fell from its previous peak, and how long it took to climb back.

## What a drawdown actually measures

A drawdown is the percentage decline from a previous all-time high to a subsequent low, before a new high is reached. If a portfolio grows to $100,000, then falls to $70,000 before eventually recovering, that's a 30% drawdown — regardless of what the portfolio does afterward.

Three numbers describe every drawdown:

- **Depth** — how far it fell (30% in the example above).
- **Length** — how many days or months it took to go from peak to trough.
- **Recovery time** — how long after the trough it took to reach a new all-time high.

## Why depth alone isn't the full picture

A 20% drawdown that recovers in three months is a very different experience than a 20% drawdown that takes three years to recover. The first is a rough quarter. The second means someone checking their portfolio regularly spent three years watching a number that never got back to where it started — even though the eventual long-term return might look fine on a 20-year chart.

This is why "worst drawdown" and "longest drawdown" are both worth checking, not just one. A strategy's *maximum* drawdown tells you the worst single hit; the recovery length tells you how long you'd need to stay disciplined through it.

## Why this matters more than average volatility

Standard deviation (a common volatility measure) describes how much an investment typically wiggles around its average. Drawdown describes the single worst real experience an investor actually lived through. For most people, it's the drawdown — not the average volatility — that determines whether they can stick with a plan.

This is also why drawdown analysis is a useful gut-check before committing to an asset. It's easy to look at a 10-year average return and feel confident. It's harder to look at "this investment lost 45% of its value and took two years to recover" and honestly ask whether you'd have held on.

## Using drawdown data well

- Look at the **worst drawdown**, not just the average return, before deciding how much of a portfolio to allocate to a volatile asset.
- Check the **recovery time**, not just the depth — a fast recovery is a very different risk than a slow one.
- Compare drawdowns **across assets** for the same historical period. Two investments can have similar long-term CAGR while one asked investors to tolerate a far rougher ride to get there.

A backtest that only reports the ending balance is hiding the part of the story that determines whether a real person would have actually stayed invested long enough to reach it.
